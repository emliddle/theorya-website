Ten HR Practices That AI Makes Obsolete

Jim Scully

Of everything AI does to human resources, obsolescence is the rarest verdict. Most methods transform — they keep their name and purpose and change the unit they run on. A few are elevated. Only a handful actually die. And when an HR practice dies, it is for one of two reasons: it was a periodic stand-in for something the organization couldn’t see in real time, or it was machinery for rationing a scarcity that no longer exists. Make the invisible continuously visible and the scarce abundant, and these ten have nothing left to do. Obsolete doesn’t mean the underlying need disappears — only that this particular mechanism does. Where the need survives, it moves somewhere better.

Proxies for what we couldn’t see

Five rituals that exist only because we couldn’t observe the real thing continuously. AI observes it directly, and the approximation becomes noise.

1. The Annual Performance Appraisal. A once-a-year verdict on behavior that happened months ago — a reported signal delivered at maximum latency. It exists because continuous observation of contribution was impossible, so we compressed a year of judgment into a single retrospective form. AI now reads performance from emitted signals as the work happens: judgment quality, escalation appropriateness, exception resolution. The need — knowing how someone is actually doing — survives and moves to continuous assessment. The annual ritual, with its recency bias and calibration theater, does not.

2. 360-Degree Feedback. The same blindness, worked from more angles. It asks colleagues to recall and rate behavioral abstractions because the organization couldn’t see collaboration directly. But actual collaboration patterns, hand-offs, and how a person’s judgment calls resolve are now emitted continuously — more reliable, more timely, and impossible to game through impression management. The 360 was a survey standing in for sensing. AI supplies the sensing, and the annual campaign to collect anonymous adjectives quietly loses its reason to exist.

3. The Employee Engagement Survey. A periodic question about how people feel, asked quarterly, under conditions that shape the answer. It was the best available proxy for a workforce’s real state. But employees bypassing the front door, attrition precursors, collaboration intensity, and sentiment captured at the point of service tell you — continuously, behaviorally — what a survey only guesses at three weeks late. The engagement survey doesn’t vanish entirely; it drops from the primary instrument to an occasional footnote, which for a practice that has anchored HR for decades amounts to the same thing.

4. The Nine-Box Grid. Performance plotted against potential, on two axes calibrated to the old world: performance meant task output, potential meant hierarchical promotability. Both now measure the wrong thing, and plotting the wrong thing with precision produces confident misallocation. Talent assessment survives — but as a continuous read of what judgment a person exercises and at what autonomy, not an annual dot on a nine-square grid that everyone re-negotiates in the calibration meeting.

5. The Exit Interview. The organization’s attempt to learn why people leave, captured at the single worst moment to ask — the last day, at lowest trust and highest social-desirability bias. Everything the exit interview hopes to discover was already emitted months earlier: engagement decline, collaboration withdrawal, utilization shifts. AI sees the departure coming and explains it better than anything a departing employee will say on the way out. The need to understand attrition rises; the exit-day conversation as the way to learn it is finished.

Rationing a scarcity that vanished

Four machines for counting, queuing, and rationing scarce labor and scarce information. When intelligence is abundant and information is instantly findable, there is nothing left to ration.

6. Headcount Planning. Counting people as the unit of capacity made sense when a head was the irreducible unit of work. When AI agents absorb execution and the real question becomes what judgment capacity you need — and in what human-AI composition — planning by headcount is like counting typewriters after word processing. Capacity planning survives and matters more than ever; the head as its unit does not.

7. The Job Requisition. A slot, justified by volume, approved, posted, and filled on a cycle measured in weeks. It assumed capacity arrived in fixed, pre-authorized units you requested in advance. In a capability-liquid model, demand for judgment pulls capability the moment it is needed — and the requisition’s approval-and-posting latency is itself the constraint the model cannot tolerate. The need to add capability persists; the requisition ritual that gates it becomes friction with no purpose.

8. The Applicant Tracking System. A pipeline built to move high volumes of candidates for execution roles through stages — a machine for rationing scarce recruiter attention across abundant applicants. When hiring shifts to judgment roles assessed by different means, and the scarce thing becomes discernment rather than throughput, a system built to track funnel stages is tracking the wrong thing entirely. A record of candidates survives; the volume-pipeline logic the ATS was built around is obsolete.

9. The Employee Handbook. A static document almost nobody read, existing because policy was scarce, hard to find, and expensive to interpret. AI makes every rule instantly findable, jurisdiction-aware, and answerable in plain language at the point of need. Policy doesn’t disappear — it becomes computable policy logic and on-demand guidance. The handbook as a bound document you are handed and told to read is finished; what replaces it answers the question you actually have.

A different kind of death

One practice doesn’t die because AI makes it obsolete. It was always obsolete — AI just removes the last excuse for pretending otherwise.

10. The Culture Initiative (with no structural change behind it). Culture is the current, not the riverbed. A values campaign, an engagement action plan, a poster about ownership — these try to change behavior without changing the structure that produces it, and the water flows back the moment the campaign ends. AI forces the real work into view: redesign the measurement frame, the authority model, the accountability structure, and the culture follows on its own. The initiative that substitutes for structural change is the most expensive obsolete practice of all — because it feels like progress while it changes nothing.

The pattern

One thread runs through all ten. Every obsolete practice was a proxy — a stand-in for a truth the organization couldn’t observe, or a ration for a resource it couldn’t afford. Make the truth continuously visible and the resource abundant, and the stand-in has nothing left to stand in for. These ten don’t need to be redesigned. They need to be retired — and the budget, meeting time, and organizational attention they quietly consume redirected to the judgment, governance, and structural design that the abundant-intelligence era actually rewards.

Obsolete isn’t a threat. It’s permission — to stop maintaining machinery for a world that no longer exists.
